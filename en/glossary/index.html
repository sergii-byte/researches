Dissertation glossary

Key concepts of the volunteer resilience study — psychological constructs, methods and related terms.

ABCDE FGHIL MNOPR STVW
A
Adaptation
Process of psychological adjustment to environmental demands. The adaptive function of volunteering reduces helplessness and supports integration of traumatic experience.
Altruism
Selfless concern for the well-being of others, expressed through real action — assistance, support, sacrifice of personal interests. In the volunteer's context: a motive that, when mature (with boundaries and meaning), nourishes rather than depletes the helper.
Altruistic orientation
The personality's drive toward prosocial behaviour through volunteering with the aim of providing selfless social help. The author's validated methodology (Barinov & Shandruk, 2025) is a 23-item instrument with test-retest reliability r = 0.78.
Anxiety (state / trait)
State anxiety — situational reaction to specific stressors. Trait anxiety — stable disposition to perceive a wide range of situations as threatening. Both correlate negatively with all resilience indicators (Spielberger–Khanin).
Authenticity
Congruence between one's experience, expression and behaviour. The third fundamental existential motivation in Längle's framework ("to be oneself"). A condition of mature, non-depleting volunteering.
Autonomy
Capacity for self-governed action grounded in one's own values rather than external pressure. One of ten indicators of personal maturity (Dembitska–Erhert).
Avoidance coping
Strategy of mentally or behaviourally distancing oneself from the stressor. A profile dominated by avoidance is associated with the lowest resilience indicators.
B
β (beta coefficient)
Standardised regression coefficient indicating the strength and direction of a predictor's contribution to the dependent variable, controlling for other predictors. Allows direct comparison of predictors measured on different scales.
Big Five
Five-factor model of personality: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism. The EPI (Eysenck) captures the neuroticism and extraversion axes used in this study.
Burnout
Emotional, physical and mental exhaustion resulting from prolonged occupational or volunteering stress. A central risk in war-time volunteering; follows a typical trajectory from enthusiasm to apathy and exit.
C
CD-RISC-10
Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale, 10-item form. Used as the integrative measure of dispositional resilience in this study.
Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT)
Therapeutic approach focused on restructuring maladaptive thoughts, beliefs and behaviours. One of the three theoretical pillars of the formation programme.
Confrontational coping
Active opposition to the stressor, often with aggressive or risk-taking features. In an isolated profile is associated with low resilience.
Constructive coping
Problem-focused strategies (planning, positive reappraisal, seeking support) directed at resolving the stressor. Dominance of constructive coping marks the highest resilience profile.
Coping strategies
Cognitive and behavioural efforts to regulate stress. The study identifies four profiles: avoidance, confrontation, distancing of responsibility, and constructive coping. The first two are inferior in resilience to the latter two.
Creativity
Capacity to find new solutions, ideas and forms of expression. One of the ten indicators of personal maturity (Dembitska–Erhert); empirically a positive predictor of openness to experience.
D
Decentration
Ability to take the perspective of another person, to step outside one's own frame of reference. One of the ten indicators of personal maturity.
Distancing (responsibility distancing)
Coping strategy in which the person mentally separates from the responsibility for a problem they cannot influence. In the study — a protective coping yielding moderate resilience, often better than avoidance or confrontation under unsolvable conditions.
Distress
Destructive form of stress, in which the load exceeds adaptive resources and harms functioning. Opposed to "eustress" (constructive mobilisation).
E
Emotional stability
The inverse of neuroticism in the Big Five. One of the key factors of resilience — positively correlated with every resilience indicator in volunteers.
EPI (Eysenck Personality Inventory)
Eysenck's questionnaire measuring extraversion–introversion and emotional stability–neuroticism. Used as a measure of neuroticism in the study.
Existential analysis
Längle's school of psychotherapy, grounded in Frankl, that focuses on authentic engagement with one's life through four fundamental motivations. One of the three theoretical sources of the formation programme.
Existentiality
Capacity for openness, decisiveness and authentic engagement with the world (Längle). Includes self-distancing, self-transcendence, existential freedom and responsibility. A powerful predictor of resilience.
F
Fundamental existential motivations
Four basic dimensions of existence in Längle's frame: support ("to be"), value ("to love"), authenticity ("to be oneself"), meaning ("to act"). All four correlate positively with volunteer resilience.
G
Grishyn's method
Method for diagnosing personal resilience, decomposing it into 8 components: stress resistance, social contact, self-control, ability to overcome difficulties, openness to experience, optimal regulation, self-determination/meaningfulness, challenge orientation.
H
Humanistic psychology
School of psychology centred on personal growth, authenticity, self-actualisation and the inherent worth of the person (Maslow, Rogers). One of the theoretical sources of the formation programme.
I
Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs)
People forced to leave their permanent residence due to armed conflict but remaining within their country. Subject of a separate study by the author on the emotional sphere of first- and second-wave IDPs (Barinov & Lisena, 2024).
Internalisation
Process by which external norms and values are made one's own. Internalised humanistic values form the most stable motivational base for volunteering.
L
Life philosophy
Conscious system of beliefs about life's meaning, values and direction. One of the ten indicators of personal maturity; strongly correlated with the challenge-orientation component of resilience.
M
M (mean)
Arithmetic mean — the sum of observed values divided by their count. The central measure of location for a sample.
Maladaptation
Disruption of adjustment to environment, with deteriorating psychological functioning. Risk factors include low resilience, high anxiety and predominance of avoidant coping.
Mann–Whitney U
Non-parametric test comparing two independent groups when distributions are not normal. Used in the study for between-group comparisons (e.g. by gender).
Moral distress
Psychological state arising when a person knows the morally right action but cannot perform it because of external constraints. A specific risk in war-time volunteering.
N
Neuroticism
Big Five factor reflecting the tendency toward negative emotions, anxiety and mood instability. One of the strongest negative predictors of resilience in this study.
O
Openness to experience
Big Five factor; component of resilience reflecting receptivity to new experience, ideas, feelings and people. Correlated with creativity and existential freedom.
P
p-value
Probability of obtaining the observed (or more extreme) result under the null hypothesis. Values p < 0.05 are conventionally considered statistically significant.
Personal maturity
Integrative characteristic of ten indicators per Dembitska–Erhert: responsibility, decentration, depth of experience, life philosophy, tolerance, autonomy, contact, self-acceptance, creativity, synergy. Critically low maturity is a key determinant of reduced resilience.
Post-traumatic growth (PTG)
Positive psychological changes developed in response to extreme stress or trauma. A potential outcome of volunteering when resilience is sufficient.
Predictor
A variable that, in regression analysis, makes a statistically significant unique contribution to predicting the dependent variable while other variables are controlled. Stronger conclusion than mere correlation.
PTSD
Post-traumatic stress disorder. A key risk in war-time volunteering, particularly without adequate preparation and support systems.
R
R² (coefficient of determination)
Proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the model's predictors. Values range from 0 to 1; reported as a measure of explanatory power in regression.
Resilience
Integrative capacity to maintain effective functioning, emotional balance and meaning-stability under prolonged volunteer activity associated with crisis and traumatic events. A dynamic mechanism of recovery and growth — not a static trait. Develops through the interaction of individual, social and contextual factors.
Responsibility
Acceptance of authorship over one's choices and their consequences. One of the ten indicators of personal maturity; the second pole of existential responsibility in Längle's framework.
S
σ (standard deviation)
Measure of dispersion of values around the mean. The larger σ, the more variable the sample. Reported as M ± σ in descriptive statistics.
Secondary traumatisation
Psychological reactions resembling PTSD that arise in the helper through prolonged contact with the suffering of others. A central occupational risk in volunteering.
Self-acceptance
Acceptance of oneself with strengths and limitations, without conditional self-worth. One of the ten indicators of personal maturity (sometimes confused with "self-perception"; in this study unified as "self-acceptance"). Strongly correlated with stress resistance.
Self-actualisation
Realisation of personal potential and authentic self-expression (Maslow). A foundational concept of humanistic psychology drawn on in the formation programme.
Self-determination
Capacity to act in accordance with one's own values and beliefs, independent of external pressure. Correlated with the meaningfulness of life and all indicators of personal maturity.
Self-regulation
Conscious management of one's emotions, behaviour and cognition to attain one's goals. A target of the regulatory module of the formation programme.
Self-transcendence
Capacity to move beyond personal interests for values, others or a higher purpose. By regression analysis, positively defines most parameters of resilience.
Social support seeking coping
Coping strategy oriented toward seeking informational, emotional or practical help from others. Empirically the strongest positive predictor of volunteer resilience in this study.
Spearman's ρ
Non-parametric rank correlation coefficient. Used when variables are not normally distributed; ranges from –1 to +1.
Stress resistance
Capacity to maintain performance and psychological balance under stressors. Strongly related to self-acceptance and existential freedom.
Synergy
Integrative quality of the personality reflecting the harmonious co-functioning of its components. One of the ten indicators of personal maturity; correlated with constructive coping.
T
Test–retest reliability
Stability of a measurement's results over time. The author's altruism methodology shows r = 0.78 — an acceptable level for psychological instruments.
Tolerance
Acceptance of differences in views, beliefs and ways of life. One of the ten indicators of personal maturity; correlated with the social-contact component of resilience.
V
Volunteering
Multi-level interaction of personal and social factors that simultaneously shapes self-development, prosocial identity and the collective resilience of the community. Under war conditions it requires high resilience as a basic resource for stress mastery.
W
WCQ (Ways of Coping Questionnaire)
Lazarus & Folkman's questionnaire measuring eight coping strategies: confrontation, distancing, self-control, social-support seeking, accepting responsibility, escape–avoidance, planful problem-solving, positive reappraisal.
